# Research — Your Brain Can Sleep While You're Still Awake

## Working thesis

Sleep and wakefulness are not perfectly global whole-brain switches. Local cortical circuits can express sleep-like ON/OFF activity during behavioral wakefulness, some species naturally sleep one hemisphere at a time, and a 2026 Nature Neuroscience study showed that deliberately inducing NREM-like cortical ON/OFF periods in awake sleep-deprived mice reduced local sleep need and restored performance on a sleep-dependent tactile-memory task.

## Strongest current evidence

### 1. 2026 induced local sleep-like activity in awake mice

NIH summary of the peer-reviewed Nature Neuroscience paper by Driessen et al. reports that researchers used optogenetic/genetic methods to induce rhythmic ON/OFF activity in cortical regions of awake sleep-deprived mice for 30 minutes. The pattern mimicked NREM slow-wave activity. After stimulation, those regions showed lower subsequent slow-wave activity during natural sleep, consistent with reduced local sleep need. Sleep-deprived mice receiving bilateral stimulation in relevant motor/sensory regions performed similarly to well-rested mice on a tactile-memory task, while unstimulated sleep-deprived mice performed worse.

Primary paper: Driessen K. et al., “Induction of cortical ON/OFF periods in awake mice fulfills sleep functions,” Nature Neuroscience (2026), DOI 10.1038/s41593-026-02318-9.

NIH: https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/researchers-trigger-sleeps-restorative-effect-parts-awake-brain

### 2. Local sleep during wakefulness is not a brand-new idea

The NIH release notes prior work showing local slow-wave activity during wakefulness in sleep-deprived rats and humans. This supports the broader idea that local neuronal populations can temporarily enter sleep-like dynamics even while the organism remains behaviorally awake. The new result matters because researchers induced a structured pattern deliberately and measured a restorative function.

Background overview: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/110427-sleep-deprived-brains-nature-science-health-rat-asleep-awake

### 3. Dolphins/cetaceans use unihemispheric sleep

NOAA states that whales and dolphins can sleep with one cerebral hemisphere at a time, preserving the ability to swim, breathe, and remain aware of their ocean environment. Classic EEG studies also directly measured unihemispheric slow-wave sleep in dolphins.

NOAA: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/pacific-islands/endangered-species-conservation/question-week-dolphins-and-whales-high-seas

PubMed dolphin EEG: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3670722/

Review: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11118608/

### 4. Birds can sleep in flight

Rattenborg et al. attached EEG recorders to great frigatebirds flying over the ocean for up to 10 days. The birds slept with one hemisphere at a time and sometimes both hemispheres simultaneously while airborne. Average sleep in flight was about 0.69 hours/day, far less than on land.

Nature Communications: https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms12468

### 5. Human sleep can show hemispheric asymmetry in an unfamiliar environment

A 2016 Current Biology study found that on the first night in an unfamiliar sleep-lab environment, one hemisphere showed reduced sleep depth in part of the default-mode network and stronger responsiveness to unusual sounds. This was described as a possible “night watch.” The asymmetry was not present in later sessions in that study.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27112296/

Important caveat: later research suggests the first-night effect is not fully explained by hemispheric asymmetry and can occur in familiar as well as unfamiliar environments. Do not present human sleep as clean dolphin-style unihemispheric sleep.

Later study: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39126649/

## Audience-demand evidence

- A public AskScience discussion asking how dolphins sleep while still needing to breathe accumulated more than 11,000 upvotes, with the unihemispheric-sleep explanation becoming the central fascination point: https://www.reddit.com/r/askscience/comments/fmxnf8/
- The exact 2026 induced-local-sleep result is fresh enough to provide novelty while the broader dolphin/half-brain premise is already proven to trigger curiosity.

Reddit is used only as qualitative demand evidence, not as a factual source.

## Fact-risk rules

1. Never say humans can currently replace normal sleep with stimulation.
2. Never imply the 2026 result was conducted in people; it was in mice.
3. Use “sleep-like activity,” “NREM-like ON/OFF periods,” or “local sleep” where precision matters.
4. Do not say the entire stimulated hemisphere was asleep; the experiment targeted cortical regions.
5. Do not describe humans as routinely sleeping one full hemisphere at a time.
6. The first-night-effect asymmetry is interesting but should be presented with the later-study caveat.
7. Frigatebirds can sleep in flight, but the original study found they slept much less in the air than on land; do not imply full nightly sleep while flying.
8. Keep the conclusion cautious: human non-invasive stimulation is a future research direction, not a demonstrated sleep substitute.

## Story escalation

1. Personal assumption: sleep is an on/off switch.
2. Break assumption: local circuits can show sleep-like activity during wakefulness.
3. Fresh experiment: deliberately induce the rhythm and recover a memory benefit in sleep-deprived mice.
4. Nature already does something more extreme: dolphins split sleep across hemispheres.
5. Birds push it further: sleep while flying.
6. Humans show hints of asymmetric/local sleep, but not the clean dolphin trick.
7. Future question: could targeted stimulation someday provide local recovery without replacing sleep?
8. Personal payoff: “awake” and “asleep” are labels for distributed brain states, not a single binary switch.
